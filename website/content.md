# MAPLE Website Content

**Development status:** This content is a working draft, not the final website content, and is still under development.

This document outlines the story, information, and resources for the MAPLE website. It describes content rather than visual design. Publish material and links only when they are ready for public release; the development repository remains private.

## 1. Introduction

**Proposed headline:** A clearer path for young creators to share their work.

MAPLE, the Minor Authors’ Public License for Expression, is an OpenRockets initiative exploring a licensing framework with young creators in mind. It aims to make authorship, attribution, and choices about sharing intellectual property easier to understand.

**Current status:** MAPLE version `0.1.0` is an unratified, non-operative working draft. It grants no rights or permissions and must not be applied to any work. Keep this status visible wherever the draft is described or made available.

## 2. Why MAPLE

Young people create software, art, writing, and other expressive work. Understanding authorship and deciding how to share that work can be difficult, particularly when copyright ownership and the legal capacity to enter an agreement are treated as the same question.

Explain the problem MAPLE is exploring, why understandable attribution and permissions matter, and why the treatment of minors can vary across jurisdictions. Describe the intended framework as work in development, without suggesting that these questions have already been resolved.

## 3. The license

Explain what a license is, the decisions MAPLE is intended to address, and how the proposed terms will be developed and reviewed. Once approved for public release, provide the exact license text with its version and status. Pair it with a clearly labeled plain-language explanation that helps readers understand the text and does not replace it.

Do not present proposed permissions, commercial terms, guardian involvement, or legal outcomes as settled before they have been decided and reflected in the license. Explain any optional supporting addendum separately from the license itself.

## 4. Versions and change history

Explain how working drafts, release candidates, and ratified releases are distinguished. Identify the current version and its status, and provide a concise history of meaningful changes as versions are approved for publication. For each published version, identify the corresponding license text, release date, and relevant research or archive record. Preserve access to earlier published versions.

## 5. Research and documentation

Introduce the white paper as the place for the research, reasoning, and policy choices behind the license. When available, connect each paper to the specific license version it discusses. Provide access to approved supporting materials, such as the versioning specification, plain-language summary, compatibility information, and project roadmap as they become available.

An FAQ and a practical guide may be added when the project is ready to explain specific questions reliably. Make clear which materials explain the license and which document contains the license terms.

## 6. Publications and archive

Present approved public publications with their titles, versions, dates, and permanent links. The planned workflow includes Zenodo records and DOIs for published materials. The license and white paper may have separate records when deposited as separate objects; identify which license version each paper discusses and link the records to one another.

Only display a DOI or archive link after the corresponding record exists. Explain that a publication or DOI alone does not ratify a draft.

## 7. Review and project progress

Explain how research, legal and community feedback can inform successive versions. When reviews are available and suitable for publication, identify the material and version reviewed, link to the review where appropriate, and summarize the resulting changes. External review is planned; do not imply that review or approval has already occurred.

Share significant milestones and future work, including developing the draft section by section, preparing the white paper, and seeking feedback. Distinguish plans from completed work and avoid implying formal recognition or certification.

## 8. Questions and practical guidance

Offer concise answers as the project matures: who the project aims to help, what the current draft's status means, where to find an approved version, how version numbers work, and where research and review records can be found. Address questions about attribution, jurisdiction, guardians, and compatibility only to the extent supported by the actual text and research.

## 9. About the project and its wider vision

Introduce MAPLE as an OpenRockets initiative, explain the project's motivation, and recognize contributors with their consent. The MAPLE Nano Card may be discussed as an exploratory idea in the wider vision, with no suggestion that a product or service is already available.
